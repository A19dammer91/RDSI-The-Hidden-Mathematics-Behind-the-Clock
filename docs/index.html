RDSI: The Hidden Mathematics Behind the Clock

Representation, Domain Modelling and Software Implementation

Start here: modulo

Modulo, the remainder after division, is famously hard to explain with formulas alone. But everyone understands a clock. It is 11 o'clock, three hours from now it is 2 o'clock. That is 11 + 3 = 14, and 14 mod 12 = 2. Once someone sees that, they understand modulo for the rest of their life.

Try it. Change the start time and the number of hours you wait.

A clock can only show 12 hours, so after every full lap of 12 it starts over. Modulo is simply the number of hours left over after the full laps. Try 12 hours later and watch what happens: the leftover is 0, but a clock never shows 0. It shows 12. Keep that in mind, we come back to it.

Two ways to build a number

Money: many answers

You have notes of €25 and €12 and want to pay exactly €575. There are two ways: 11 notes of €25 plus 25 of €12, or 23 notes of €25 and none of €12. Mathematicians call this a linear Diophantine equation: which amounts of each kind land exactly on the total?

The clock: one answer

T = 3600·H + 60·M + S

Same shape, but exactly one answer. The difference is the structure. The clock's bases form a chain where each is an exact multiple of the one below, and every coefficient is bounded:

1000 → 60 → 60 → 24

Divisibility plus bounds gives exactly one representation per instant. Remove either, and uniqueness is gone.

Both are Diophantine. Neither is a mistake: money is built to explore many combinations, the clock to guarantee certainty.

0-based or 1-based?

Remember the 12 that a clock shows instead of 0? Most software counts positions from 0 to q − 1. Then the value that ends a cycle is also the value that starts the next one: midnight is both the end of the day and the beginning of the next. Programmers know the result as the off-by-one error.

The RDSI paper shows that counting 1 to q, that is p ≡ 1 (mod q), keeps end and beginning apart. Same arithmetic, different model. For q = 12:

Your turn

Try these in your head first, then open the answer.

1. It is 9 o'clock. What time is it 5 hours later?

9 + 5 = 14, and 14 mod 12 = 2. It is 2 o'clock.

2. What is 40 mod 12?

40 = 3 × 12 + 4. Three full laps, 4 left over. So 40 mod 12 = 4.

3. What is 24 mod 12, and what does a clock show?

24 = 2 × 12 + 0, so the remainder is 0. A 1-based clock shows 12: the end of a lap, not the start of one.

4. You type 130 into Sec. What do Min and Sec show?

130 = 2 × 60 + 10. Two full minutes carry over, 10 seconds are left. Min = 2, Sec = 10.

Why it matters

Everywhere a big number becomes something readable, the same pattern appears: 3,661 seconds is 1:01:01, 20260917 is 17 September 2026, 1,234,567 bytes is about 1.23 MB. Layers, laps, leftovers. Every system with layers chooses: make every combination easy to find, like money, or make uncertainty impossible, like a clock.
